Designingfor peoplewho would rather just talk

Two prototypes. About ten minutes. Delphi is a speaker that lives in an older person's home, with no screen and no camera. The Delphi Family app, its companion, keeps relatives connected and makes aiding in companionship a group effort. Introductions is a feature which helps one Delphi user meet another, they're a generation that deeply valued community.

The family’s view.

01 · WHO SHE TALKS TO

Someone can talk to Delphi every day and still have very little contact with another person.

Family may live far away. Friends move, slow down, or die. Retirement and bereavement take away the reasons to leave the house or pick up the phone.

When a conversation suggests someone the person might enjoy talking to, Delphi can offer the introduction once. On a social platform, the person has to find someone, make contact and arrange the conversation themselves.

PRECEDENT
Age UK, Call in Time

Weekly calls between older people and volunteers, matched through a sign-up process. Introductions works between Delphi users instead, from what each has already chosen to share, with no profile to fill in.

05 · BEFORE YOU GO

Why both?

What Delphi learns first is what someone loves, and the best person to share that with is often another Delphi user. Introductions is that step.

The family app comes first, because families are a big part of how a product like Delphi grows. Emma can check on her mum without being invasive, the care of an older adult is shared between relatives instead of falling to the favourite child, and they can see their mother is being social beyond the family without hearing what she said.

The family app is to show Introductions in context, and as the other arm of what technology can do: keep someone company, and help them find company.

My role. An AI companion that talks to someone every day can still leave them lonely. I designed Introductions so Delphi can connect similar users without the hassle of social profiles. I designed Delphi Family so relatives know when something needs attention without the care falling on one person or her conversations being reported.

APPENDIX
Notes on the family app

The promise

This app is the family's screen. It tells them when something needs attention and leaves the day's conversations with Margaret. Every screen carries one promise: peace of mind with her privacy intact.

Three decisions that shaped it

  • Memory Book belongs to Margaret. The stories are in her own words and she decides what is kept, by voice. Family reads and listens. She stays the only author.
  • Alerts record who reached out. Each card says who acted and how. The app does not note down who looked, because read receipts would turn a calm app into a scoreboard between siblings. Anything older than a week settles into History.
  • Delphi always says what she is doing. A wellness check moves from "Delphi is checking in" to "Margaret is fine" or "Couldn't reach her", and a failure always offers Try again and Call Margaret.
More decisions
  • The badge lights for urgent alone. A badge promises something deserves an interruption. Reserving it for urgent keeps that promise believable. Amber items wait inside the tab.
  • Call Margaret lives on Today. Inside an alert it would suggest calling is for emergencies. On Today it says a check-in is something families do on any day.
  • Introductions lives inside Today. A tab would frame it as a signal that rises and falls. Inside Today it says what happened and stops.
  • Family splits into two decisions. My notifications affect only me. Who has access affects Margaret and the whole family, so adding someone needs her approval.
  • Escalation is visible step by step. If Margaret doesn't answer, Delphi calls her care contact and the family sees each step. If that fails too, the next move is theirs: call the contact, call Margaret, or try again.
  • Today shows how she is doing. Last spoke with Delphi, a good time to call, something to ask about from her own Memory Book, and who last called. Each helps family connect. None says what she said.

What the research says

  • Most caregiver apps are care hubs: task lists, vitals, shared logs. In the caregiver-app reviews I read, the real problem is handoffs, where information gets lost between people. Delphi Family is small on purpose and answers one question well.
  • Both generations name privacy as their top concern, but they weigh monitoring differently. In a small US study of 18 mothers and 10 of their adult children (Berridge and Wetle, The Gerontologist, 2020), the children preferred every monitoring technology more than their mothers did and underestimated how much say the mothers should have. The sample was Meals on Wheels clients, so I read it as a direction, not a rate. It is why Margaret approves who has access, and why family gets a count of calls and never a word she said.
  • Alerts that cry wolf lose the home, so severity has to be rare to be believed.
  • Next I would sit with an older adult at home, watch how she responds when Delphi mentions a family check-in, and rework this section around what I see. I would also test:
    • The two hour wait after Margaret answers a check-in, and the confirm step. Both are my best guesses, and real users will show where the number belongs.
    • Whether siblings need more than handled notes to hand off. I would ask before designing anything.
    • Whether Margaret should see handled notes. They are family-only today.

Visual system

Green marks anything tappable. Red and amber are reserved for alert severity, and handled states pair colour with a tick or a word. Delphi is a stand-in name, so I kept the system to what the prototype needed and left brand identity out of scope.

What family sees

Family sees one number, and only if Margaret agrees: how many social calls she has had. She chooses that when she says yes to Introductions and can turn it off by telling Delphi.

“Would you like Emma and David to see how many calls you’ve had? Only the number, never what was said.”

What is said on a call belongs to the two people on it. The one exception is a short list of danger phrases, which Delphi listens for.

What I left out on purpose

Most of the work was deciding what not to build. These all came up, and I cut each one for a reason.

  • A family notes space. Siblings do lose information between them, and a shared notes feed would fix that. It would also turn into a running log about Margaret, which is the care hub pattern I was trying to avoid. It would show which sibling writes and which doesn't, the same scoreboard I kept out of Alerts. Handled notes already cover the useful part: one pick and one line, stuck to the alert it belongs to. If families want more, I would grow that before adding a place to write about her. Before building it I would ask how siblings tell each other they are worried today. If the answer is a group chat and it works, there is nothing to build.
  • Summaries of what she said. Family gets a count of calls and nothing else. Even a line like "she seemed low today" turns her conversations into a report on her.
  • Family writing in Memory Book. It is her book, so there is no add button and no topic suggestions until families ask.
  • Vitals, tasks and shared logs. That space is crowded, and it is not what Delphi is. The app answers one question: is she okay, and do I need to do anything?
  • An approval step before marking something handled. In an emergency nobody should be waiting on permission. Only adding or removing people needs the Organiser, and Margaret can remove anyone by telling Delphi.
  • Browsing in Introductions. No profiles, no list of people, no choice of who. Delphi offers one person, once, and each of them says yes or no in private.
  • Alert categories per person. A son who wants falls but not mood dips cannot say so yet. I don't know which splits families actually want, and a settings screen full of toggles would be a guess.
  • A screen for Margaret. Delphi has no screen and no camera. Everything Margaret controls, she does by talking to it.
